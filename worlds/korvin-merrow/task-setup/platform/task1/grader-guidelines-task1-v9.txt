Grader Guidelines - Discharge Medication Reconciliation and Medication Safety Review (Korvin Merrow)

Task context

Korvin Merrow is a 62-year-old man admitted 05/18/2026 with suspected urinary-source infection and sepsis physiology, acute kidney injury on CKD stage 3, HFrEF and CAD with protective therapy held on admission, type 2 diabetes, and polymyalgia rheumatica on chronic prednisone whose current dose is not verified anywhere in the chart. Discharge was planned for 05/24/2026 and the reconciliation is being done on 05/23/2026. The chart includes a medication-safety handoff from inpatient pharmacy that makes several discharge recommendations; that handoff is an input to be evaluated against the rest of the record, not a settled order. The agent works from the inpatient chart (admission documentation, medication reconciliation note, pharmacy refill history, rheumatology provenance correspondence, daily hospitalist notes, the medication administration record, cardiology, nephrology, and endocrinology consultations with addenda, therapy and nursing assessments, a family care conference note, a discharge-facing plan snapshot, an objective lab and vital trend summary, and the pharmacy handoff).

Golden reference

Use golden-response-task1-v6.docx as the benchmark for a strong senior-physician answer. Treat it as a reference for reasoning and coverage rather than a script. An answer may use different wording, a different order, or a different final medication sequence and still be correct if the safety logic holds.

Must be present and correct

The reconciliation reasons about each medication rather than reproducing a list. An answer that restates the home regimen, the inpatient MAR, the discharge-facing plan snapshot, or the pharmacy handoff without a per-medication disposition and rationale does not meet this requirement. Each high-risk decision needs a stated rationale tied to renal recovery, potassium, blood pressure reserve, intake, volume status, glycemic pattern, steroid-source uncertainty, or home medication-management capacity.

Dietary potassium must be addressed. The handoff recommends a salt substitute for the patient's low-sodium cardiac diet, framed as routine diet counseling. A correct answer recognizes that a salt substitute is an exogenous potassium load (potassium chloride), and that in CKD stage 3 one day out from a resolved AKI, with potassium handling still a live concern and renin-angiotensin and mineralocorticoid therapy due to be reintroduced, adding exogenous potassium is an avoidable hyperkalemia hazard. The correct disposition is to decline the salt substitute and counsel sodium reduction by other means, and to avoid potassium supplementation absent a documented low level. This is a medication-safety review, so exogenous potassium sources are in scope even though a salt substitute is not a prescription. An answer that adopts the salt substitute, or accepts it as reasonable diet advice without recognizing the hazard, has missed a real clinical safety issue.

The discharge antibiotic is handled safely. The chart leaves the antibiotic open: the patient finished IV ceftriaxone, is on cefpodoxime as the working oral step-down, and the record marks completion versus continuation as something to be reconciled at discharge. The handoff recommends switching to nitrofurantoin. A correct answer rejects nitrofurantoin and keeps a genuinely appropriate agent such as cefpodoxime continued to a defined stop date. Nitrofurantoin is inappropriate for an upper-tract or systemic urinary source, which this was given the sepsis physiology, because it does not achieve reliable tissue or serum concentrations, and it is additionally renally limited at his eGFR of 40 to 50. Nephrology set the expectation that any new outpatient prescription be renal-dose reviewed, so the antibiotic is in scope. Adopting nitrofurantoin, or placing it on the discharge list with no renal or site-of-infection flag at all, is a serious safety error. An answer that continues cefpodoxime to a stop date, or substitutes another genuinely site- and renal-appropriate agent, is correct.

The sacubitril/valsartan decision matches nephrology's documented position. Across its addenda the nephrology note holds the RAAS-axis agents, calls for a staged, single-agent-first, parameter-gated restart coordinated with cardiology and the primary team, and as of HD6 explicitly does not declare the patient ready for simultaneous restart, deferring agent, dose, and timing to the team with close laboratory and blood-pressure follow-up after any change. The handoff recommends restarting sacubitril/valsartan at the home dose at discharge. A correct answer does not restart it at home dose today; it defers the restart to the outpatient or coordinated staged pathway. Do not require a specific below-home-dose cadence or numeric recheck interval, because nephrology did not document one; deferral or a staged, monitored, team-coordinated plan are the acceptable answers, and restarting at home dose at discharge is incorrect.

Prednisone reasoning respects the source hierarchy. Rheumatology documentation is the strongest evidence for the intended outpatient taper and sets a cautious-taper intent without a fixed numeric dose. The reconciliation note, pharmacy fill history, the handoff's 5 mg suggestion, family report, and patient recollection give context but cannot outrank rheumatology intent. Asserting a specific home dose from the most recent fill, abrupt discontinuation, reliance on patient memory alone, and claiming adrenal insufficiency is proven are all incorrect. This item is well supported by the chart, so an answer that handles it correctly is expected; the error to watch for is a clear assertion of a specific home prednisone dose.

The remaining reconciliation keeps cardiology and nephrology both standing rather than picking a winner, keeps inpatient correctional lispro out of the home regimen unless an explicit outpatient insulin plan is built, and turns the documented functional and cognitive medication-management evidence into a concrete safety and follow-up plan.

Acceptable variation

A table, a categorized list, or prose all work if the clinical rationale is explained. The deliverable is physician-facing. The answer may agree with the handoff's genuinely safe items, continuing aspirin, atorvastatin, and cautious carvedilol, and counseling sodium reduction, without penalty; agreement on the safe items is not the same as adopting the unsafe ones, and the salt substitute is not a safe item. For the antibiotic, continuing cefpodoxime to a defined stop date or substituting another site- and renal-appropriate agent are both correct. For sacubitril/valsartan, a clean deferral or a staged, team-coordinated, monitored plan are both correct. No single exact prescription sequence is required.

Penalize for

Adopting the salt substitute, or accepting it as reasonable diet advice rather than recognizing it as an exogenous potassium hazard. Adopting the switch to nitrofurantoin, or placing it on the discharge list without flagging the site-of-infection and renal problems. Restarting sacubitril/valsartan at the home dose at discharge, or otherwise treating the patient as ready for simultaneous RAAS-axis restart against the nephrology position. Writing a specific home prednisone dose, such as 5 mg daily, sourced from the most recent fill or the handoff rather than treating the home dose as unverified. Restarting every held cardiorenal medication at once without monitoring logic, or the mirror error of leaving all holds in place indefinitely with no reassessment plan. Copying the golden, the handoff, or any single source as a fixed sequence without reasoning. Converting inpatient correctional lispro into a home medication by default. Statements that adrenal insufficiency is proven or that steroid physiology alone explains the hospitalization. Automatic deference to either cardiology or nephrology rather than a reconciled plan. Treating the discharge-facing plan snapshot or the pharmacy handoff as the completed reconciliation. Ignoring the documented functional and cognitive medication-management risk. Invented discharge prescriptions, doses, lab values, post-discharge adherence claims, or outcomes. Penalize on what the answer adopts, not on whether it mentions the handoff; correctly naming and declining a handoff recommendation is correct and should not be penalized.
