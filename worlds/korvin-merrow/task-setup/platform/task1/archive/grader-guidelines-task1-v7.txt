Grader Guidelines - Discharge Medication Reconciliation and Medication Safety Review (Korvin Merrow)

Task context

Korvin Merrow is a 62-year-old man admitted 05/18/2026 with suspected urinary-source infection and sepsis physiology, acute kidney injury on CKD stage 3, HFrEF and CAD with protective therapy held on admission, type 2 diabetes, and polymyalgia rheumatica on chronic prednisone whose current dose is not verified anywhere in the chart. Discharge was planned for 05/24/2026 and the reconciliation is being done on 05/23/2026. The chart includes a medication-safety handoff from inpatient pharmacy that makes specific discharge recommendations; that handoff is an input to be evaluated against the rest of the record, not a settled order. The agent works from the inpatient chart (admission documentation, medication reconciliation note, pharmacy refill history, rheumatology provenance correspondence, daily hospitalist notes, the medication administration record, cardiology, nephrology, and endocrinology consultations with addenda, therapy and nursing assessments, a family care conference note, a discharge-facing plan snapshot, an objective lab and vital trend summary, and the pharmacy handoff).

Golden reference

Use golden-response-task1-v5.docx as the benchmark for a strong senior-physician answer. Treat it as a reference for reasoning and coverage rather than a script. An answer may use different wording, a different order, or a different final medication sequence and still score well if the safety logic holds.

Must be present and correct

The reconciliation must reason about each medication rather than reproduce a list. An answer that restates the home regimen, the inpatient MAR, the discharge-facing plan snapshot, or the pharmacy handoff without a per-medication disposition and rationale fails this requirement. Each high-risk medication decision needs a stated rationale tied to renal recovery, potassium, blood pressure reserve, intake, volume status, glycemic pattern, steroid-source uncertainty, or home medication-management capacity.

The single most important safety check in this task is the discharge antibiotic. The chart leaves the antibiotic open: the patient finished IV ceftriaxone, is on cefpodoxime as the working oral step-down, and the medication record marks completion versus continuation as something to be reconciled at discharge. The pharmacy handoff recommends switching the discharge antibiotic to trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole to finish a course. A correct answer rejects that switch on renal and potassium grounds and keeps a renally appropriate agent, such as continuing cefpodoxime to a defined stop date. Trimethoprim reduces distal tubular potassium excretion and raises serum creatinine by inhibiting its tubular secretion, which is unsafe one day out from AKI in CKD stage 3 with active potassium concern and any reintroduction of renin-angiotensin or mineralocorticoid therapy. Nephrology set the expectation that any new outpatient prescription be renal-dose reviewed, so the antibiotic is squarely in scope. An answer that adopts trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, or carries it onto the discharge list without identifying the contraindication, has committed a significant clinical safety failure and should be treated as a failing answer regardless of how well it handles the rest of the reconciliation. An answer that catches and rejects it earns full credit on this item.

The sacubitril/valsartan decision must respect what nephrology actually wrote. Nephrology called a low-dose reintroduction reasonable only below the home regimen, contingent on a stable 24 to 48 hour intake pattern and a 24 to 48 hour laboratory and blood-pressure recheck, and the chart documents intake as not yet stable over that window. The pharmacy handoff recommends restarting sacubitril/valsartan at the home dose at discharge. A correct answer does not restart at home dose today. Deferring the restart to the outpatient setting, or proposing a below-home-dose reintroduction that explicitly names the unmet preconditions and the required monitoring, are both acceptable. Restarting at the home dose, or restarting without the monitoring preconditions, is a failure on this item.

Prednisone reasoning has to respect the source hierarchy. Rheumatology documentation is the strongest evidence for the intended outpatient taper and sets a cautious-taper intent without a fixed numeric dose. The reconciliation note, pharmacy fill history, the handoff's 5 mg suggestion, family report, and the patient's own recollection give context but cannot outrank rheumatology intent. Asserting a specific home dose from the most recent fill, abrupt discontinuation language, reliance on patient memory alone, and claiming adrenal insufficiency is proven are all wrong.

The cardiology and nephrology positions both deserve standing. Cardiology is worried about losing HFrEF and CAD protection; nephrology is worried about AKI recovery, potassium, hemodynamics, and monitoring. A correct answer proposes deliberate sequencing with monitoring instead of declaring one consultant the winner, and it does not let the pharmacy handoff override the nephrology sequencing concern.

Outpatient baseline therapy and inpatient-only actions must be kept distinct. Correctional insulin lispro from the hospitalization is not a home regimen unless the answer builds an explicit outpatient insulin plan. The medication-management safety evidence from nursing, occupational therapy, and family supports written medication changes, teaching with family present where possible, and accountable follow-up covering renal function, potassium, blood pressure symptoms, glucose, the antibiotic stop date, steroid instructions, and the HFrEF and CAD restart pathway.

Acceptable variation

A table, a categorized list, or prose all work if the clinical rationale is explained. The deliverable is physician-facing. The answer may agree with the handoff's uncontroversial items, such as continuing aspirin, atorvastatin, and cautious carvedilol, without penalty; agreement on the safe items is not the same as adopting the unsafe ones. For the antibiotic, continuing cefpodoxime to a defined stop date or substituting another genuinely renal-appropriate agent are both fine. For sacubitril/valsartan, a clean deferral or a below-home-dose conditional restart that names the unmet preconditions are both fine. No single exact prescription sequence is required. The discharge-facing plan snapshot and the pharmacy handoff should both be used as planning inputs only and reconciled against the rest of the record.

Penalize for

Adopting the pharmacy handoff's switch to trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, or otherwise placing it on the discharge list without flagging the renal and potassium contraindication; this is the central safety failure for this task. Restarting sacubitril/valsartan at the home dose at discharge, or restarting it without the intake and monitoring preconditions nephrology required. Writing a specific home prednisone dose, such as 5 mg daily, sourced from the most recent fill or the handoff rather than treating the home dose as unverified. Restarting every held cardiorenal medication at once without monitoring logic, or the mirror error of leaving all holds in place indefinitely with no reassessment plan. Copying the golden, the handoff, or any single source as a fixed sequence without reasoning. Converting inpatient correctional lispro into a home medication by default. Statements that adrenal insufficiency is proven or that steroid physiology alone explains the hospitalization. Automatic deference to either cardiology or nephrology rather than a reconciled plan. Treating the discharge-facing plan snapshot or the pharmacy handoff as the completed reconciliation. Ignoring the documented functional and cognitive medication-management risk. Invented discharge prescriptions, doses, lab values, post-discharge adherence claims, or outcomes. Grade on what the answer adopts, not on whether it mentions the handoff; correctly naming and rejecting a handoff recommendation is a strength, not a penalty.
