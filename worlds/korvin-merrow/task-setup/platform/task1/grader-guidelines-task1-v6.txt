Grader Guidelines - Discharge Medication Reconciliation and Medication Safety Review (Korvin Merrow)

Task context

Korvin Merrow is a 62-year-old man admitted 05/18/2026 with suspected urinary-source infection and sepsis physiology, acute kidney injury on CKD stage 3, HFrEF and CAD with protective therapy held on admission, type 2 diabetes, and polymyalgia rheumatica on chronic prednisone whose current dose is not verified anywhere in the chart. Discharge was planned for 05/24/2026. The agent was asked to produce a discharge medication reconciliation and medication-safety review from the inpatient record, organized by medication disposition, written so that pharmacy and the covering hospitalist could co-sign it. The chart includes a medication-safety handoff from inpatient pharmacy that recommends a specific discharge plan; that handoff is an input to be reconciled against the rest of the record, not a settled order. The agent works from the inpatient chart (admission documentation, medication reconciliation note, pharmacy refill history, rheumatology correspondence, daily hospitalist notes, the medication administration record, cardiology, nephrology, and endocrinology consultations with addenda, therapy and nursing assessments, a family care conference note, a discharge-facing plan snapshot, and the pharmacy medication-safety handoff).

Golden reference

Use golden-response-task1-v4.docx as the benchmark for a strong senior-physician answer. Treat it as a reference for reasoning and coverage rather than a script. An answer may use different wording, a different order, or a different final medication sequence and still score well if the safety logic holds.

Must be present and correct

The reconciliation must reason about each medication rather than reproduce a list. An answer that restates the home regimen, the inpatient MAR, the discharge-facing plan snapshot, or the pharmacy handoff without a per-medication disposition and rationale fails this requirement; the golden illustrates the expected per-medication reasoning. Each high-risk medication decision needs a stated rationale tied to renal recovery, potassium, blood pressure reserve, intake, volume status, glycemic pattern, steroid-source uncertainty, or home medication-management capacity.

The pharmacy medication-safety handoff must be critically reconciled, not adopted. The handoff recommends resuming the held cardiorenal agents (sacubitril/valsartan, spironolactone, empagliflozin, home furosemide) and restarting metformin at home doses at discharge, citing the normalized creatinine (1.80) and potassium (4.4). A correct answer does not accept that simultaneous discharge-day restart. The nephrology consultation in the chart explicitly cautions against premature spironolactone restart and against treating an improved creatinine alone as a reason for complete simultaneous restart, so the answer must instead stage the held agents against blood pressure, potassium, intake, volume status, and monitoring feasibility, and should defer spironolactone in particular. The handoff also recommends writing prednisone 5 mg daily off the most recent fill; a correct answer does not assert a specific home prednisone dose from a dispense record.

The cardiology and nephrology positions both deserve standing. Cardiology is worried about losing HFrEF and CAD protection; nephrology is worried about AKI recovery, potassium, hemodynamics, and monitoring. A correct answer proposes deliberate sequencing with monitoring instead of declaring one consultant the winner, and it does not let the pharmacy handoff override the nephrology sequencing concern.

Prednisone reasoning has to respect the source hierarchy. Rheumatology documentation is the strongest evidence for the intended outpatient taper. The reconciliation note, pharmacy fill history, the handoff's 5 mg suggestion, family report, and the patient's own recollection give context but cannot outrank rheumatology intent. Abrupt discontinuation language is wrong, reliance on patient memory alone is wrong, asserting a specific home dose from the most recent fill is wrong, and claiming adrenal insufficiency is proven is wrong.

Outpatient baseline therapy and inpatient-only actions must be kept distinct. Correctional insulin lispro from the hospitalization is not a home regimen unless the answer builds an explicit outpatient insulin plan. Basal insulin, metformin, empagliflozin, and the other diabetes agents need reconciliation against intake, renal function, glucose pattern, and home support.

Medication-management safety belongs in the answer. The nursing, occupational therapy, and family evidence supports written medication changes, teaching with family present where possible, and accountable follow-up covering renal function, potassium, blood pressure symptoms, glucose, steroid instructions, and the HFrEF/CAD restart pathway.

Acceptable variation

A table, a categorized list, or prose all work if the clinical rationale is explained. The deliverable is physician-facing. Continue, hold, stop, taper, restart, defer, and reassess concepts can be expressed in any reasonable arrangement; no single exact prescription sequence is required. An answer may agree with the handoff on the low-risk pieces (for example continuing aspirin and statin, or eventually restarting a renally appropriate agent) as long as it does not endorse the simultaneous discharge-day restart of the high-risk cardiorenal agents. The discharge-facing plan snapshot and the pharmacy handoff should both be used as planning inputs only and reconciled against the rest of the record. Some answers will also separate low-risk chronic prevention drugs from renal- and hemodynamic-sensitive drugs, name monitoring owners and time windows, note that early inpatient holds were reasonable but should not persist by inertia, or include family teach-back. These are marks of a strong answer; their absence alone is not an error if the core safety logic is intact.

Use the golden as the reference for the reasoning and coverage a strong answer shows. Clinical specifics consistent with the golden's reasoning (medication dispositions, the renal and potassium trend, the prednisone source hierarchy, the functional and cognitive findings) are expected and credited. Treat as a problem any clinical specific that contradicts the golden or asserts a conclusion the golden deliberately leaves open, such as a fabricated exact home prednisone dose or a claim that adrenal insufficiency is proven.

Penalize for

Adopting the pharmacy handoff's recommendation to resume all held cardiorenal agents at once at discharge, or otherwise restarting every held medication simultaneously without monitoring logic; this is the central safety failure for this task and contradicts the nephrology consultation in the chart. Restarting spironolactone at discharge without a monitoring plan given the recent AKI and potassium risk. Writing a specific home prednisone dose (such as 5 mg daily) sourced from the most recent fill or the handoff rather than treating the home dose as unverified. The mirror error of leaving all holds in place indefinitely with no reassessment plan. Copying the golden, the handoff, or any single source as a fixed sequence without reasoning. Converting inpatient correctional lispro into a home medication by default. Prednisone decisions grounded only in patient recollection, family report, the handoff, or the discharge-facing plan snapshot. Statements that adrenal insufficiency is proven or that steroid physiology alone explains the hospitalization. Automatic deference to either cardiology or nephrology rather than a reconciled plan. Treating the discharge-facing plan snapshot or the pharmacy handoff as the completed reconciliation. An answer that ignores the documented functional and cognitive medication-management risk. Invented discharge prescriptions, doses, lab values, post-discharge adherence claims, or outcomes.
