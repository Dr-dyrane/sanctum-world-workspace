## Expected Senior-Clinician Output

The discharge-readiness and care-coordination assessment should conclude that discharge planning is clinically plausible but not self-justifying. The key expected stance is that medical improvement must be reconciled with functional capacity, cognitive and medication-management vulnerability, family baseline knowledge, service feasibility, and the incompleteness of the discharge-facing snapshot.

The assessment should recognize the discharge-planning positives. Korvin is improved compared with admission: infection-oriented markers and fever burden improved, creatinine improved toward baseline, potassium improved, floor hemodynamics were stable enough for reassessment, oral intake and alertness improved, and he participated with nursing, therapy, consultants, and discharge-planning discussions. These findings make discharge planning reasonable to continue.

The assessment should then identify why transition safety remains unresolved. Baseline sources describe a patient who was independent with basic activities, occasionally used a cane, had only mild age-related forgetfulness, and participated in medication routines with family oversight. Current nursing, PT, and OT sources show improvement but not a clear return to that baseline: supervision or light assistance for transfers depending on fatigue, short-distance ambulation with guarding or assistive-device planning, endurance limitations, cueing needs, and vulnerability during multi-step ADL or medication tasks.

The assessment should treat family concerns as meaningful, not generic resistance. Mara Merrow reported that Korvin was "better than Monday" but still not ready to manage the next steps without support. She asked practical questions about medication education, heart/kidney medication changes, prednisone instructions, first-days-at-home supervision, and what to do if weakness or confusion recurs. The output should also preserve the primary team's defensible position: the patient improved enough that transition planning could reasonably be explored.

Care coordination should focus on open items rather than issuing a final order. The expected assessment should call out the need to confirm family supervision capacity, review therapy and nursing input for the selected setting, clarify assistive-device plan, decide whether home health nursing and/or therapy is feasible if discharge proceeds, arrange follow-up, and ensure medication education has an accountable owner. It should mention that FI-S03 adds logistics texture but does not complete the service plan.

Medication complexity should be included as a discharge-readiness issue. The output should note that renal/HF medication sequencing, prednisone-source coherence, diabetes medication decisions, inpatient-only insulin actions, and family-supported medication education all affect whether the plan is practical at home.

FI-W22 should be described as useful but incomplete. It lists reasonable items to complete before final discharge order entry, but it does not prove those items were completed, accepted, authorized, or sufficient. The assessment should therefore avoid treating FI-W22 as a final discharge authorization.

## Expected Shape

A clinically useful assessment should include:

- current medical stability and improvement signals;
- functional and cognitive status compared with baseline;
- medication-management and education risk;
- family/caregiver capacity and concerns;
- services, equipment, transportation, and follow-up logistics;
- gaps in the visible discharge-facing snapshot;
- a physician-facing synthesis of what must be resolved before the plan is coherent.

## Prohibited Drift

The output should not issue a final discharge order, approve services, declare discharge definitively safe or unsafe, create new post-discharge facts, blame the primary team, or expose internal implementation labels or scoring logic.
