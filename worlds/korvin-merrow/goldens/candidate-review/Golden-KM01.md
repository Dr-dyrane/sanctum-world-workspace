## Physician Response

Korvin's discharge medication reconciliation should not be a copy-forward of either the home list or the inpatient MAR. The safer approach is a source-aware, staged reconciliation that preserves chronic cardiovascular protection while accounting for AKI recovery, potassium risk, poor intake, hemodynamic reserve, diabetes variability, steroid-source uncertainty, cognition, and family support.

The chronic CAD/HFrEF medications need explicit disposition-by-medication documentation. Aspirin and atorvastatin should generally be continued unless a new contraindication is identified. The remote PCI history supports the importance of chronic CAD prevention, but it does not decide acute restart timing for renal- or hemodynamic-sensitive agents.

The early inpatient holds of sacubitril/valsartan, spironolactone, empagliflozin, metformin, and diuretic therapy were clinically reasonable during acute illness, poor intake, borderline hemodynamics, AKI on CKD stage 3, and initial potassium vulnerability. By HD6, creatinine had improved toward baseline and potassium had improved, so those admission holds should be actively reassessed rather than passively continued. That improvement does not justify restarting every renal- and hemodynamic-sensitive medication at once.

For HFrEF/CAD therapy, carvedilol can be continued or cautiously restarted if blood pressure, symptoms, and heart rate support it. Sacubitril/valsartan should be considered for staged restart only if blood pressure, renal trend, potassium, intake, and monitoring logistics are acceptable. Spironolactone should be held or deferred unless potassium and renal monitoring are reliable, because recent AKI and hyperkalemia risk remain relevant. Furosemide should be individualized to volume status, weight trend, intake, congestion symptoms, and orthostatic reserve rather than reflexively resumed or stopped. Empagliflozin should remain held until acute illness and poor intake have clearly resolved and renal status is stable enough for outpatient use. Metformin should only be resumed if renal function is stable near baseline, intake is adequate, and no acute illness contraindication remains.

Diabetes medications need the same reconciliation discipline. Basal insulin should be reconciled against outpatient use, inpatient glucose pattern, intake, and home ability to follow instructions. Correctional lispro used in the hospital should not be converted into a home regimen unless a separate final outpatient insulin plan is intentionally created and taught.

Prednisone requires special caution. The best source for intended outpatient taper is rheumatology documentation. Medication reconciliation and pharmacy history support chronic and recent exposure, but they do not prove actual adherence. Family report and patient recollection are clinically useful but lower-authority and internally uncertain. The discharge medication plan should avoid abrupt prednisone discontinuation, should not rely on the patient's memory alone, and should clearly state the intended prednisone instruction and follow-up owner. The chart supports adrenal-suppression risk as plausible; it does not prove adrenal insufficiency as the sole explanation for the hospitalization.

Gabapentin and other symptom-support medications should be reviewed for cognition, fall risk, renal function, and benefit. Alendronate, calcium/vitamin D, pantoprazole, bowel medications, and iron should be reconciled as chronic supportive therapies without distracting from the high-risk medication decisions.

The discharge-facing plan snapshot is helpful but incomplete. Before discharge, the medication reconciliation should specify, in writing, which medications are continued, restarted, held, dose-adjusted, or deferred, and why. Family should be present for teaching if possible because nursing, OT, and family documentation show improved but still vulnerable medication-management capacity under fatigue or complexity. The plan should include accountable follow-up for BMP/creatinine/potassium, blood pressure or orthostatic symptoms, glucose monitoring, heart-failure symptoms, steroid instructions, and early clinician review.

This answer leaves room for more than one defensible final medication sequence. The key safety requirement is that the sequence be intentional, monitored, source-aware, and clearly communicated rather than an automatic restart, an automatic permanent hold, or a prednisone decision based on low-authority recollection.
