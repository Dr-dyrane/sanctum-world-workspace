1.1 Big Picture Summary
Korvin Merrow is a 62-year-old man admitted after about three weeks of progressive weakness, poor oral intake, lightheadedness with a near-fall, family-noticed confusion, possible urinary symptoms, and medication-management mistakes. He has common but high-stakes chronic disease: HFrEF, CAD with remote PCI/stent, CKD stage 3, type 2 diabetes, PMR with chronic prednisone exposure, neuropathy, OSA, anemia of CKD, steroid-related bone disease, and polypharmacy.
The initial ED and admission frame is suspected urinary-source infection with dehydration/volume vulnerability, altered baseline mental status, functional decline, and AKI-on-CKD risk. Sepsis-oriented management is clinically reasonable. The hard part is that partial improvement does not make the patient safely solved. By HD6 he looks better, but discharge safety depends on reconciling renal and cardiac medication timing, prednisone-source reliability, functional recovery, family concerns, consultant recommendations, and home support. The world closes on 05/23/2026 at 18:00 during discharge planning. The downstream tasks ask for physician-facing work that a careful hospital clinician, primary care clinician, transition team, or medical director could reasonably produce: medication reconciliation, discharge summary, care coordination, consultant synthesis, early follow-up assessment, and safety/readmission-risk review. 1.5 Clinical Complexity Overview
The difficulty comes from realistic hospital medicine rather than a rare diagnosis. A correct response has to preserve mixed physiology: infection may be real and improving, but CKD/HF interaction, steroid exposure, medication effects, neuropathy, anemia, OSA, deconditioning, poor intake, and family-observed functional decline all remain clinically meaningful. Difficulty distribution across the six tasks: 2 Hard (Tasks 1 and 4), 2 Medium-Hard (Tasks 2 and 6), 2 Medium (Tasks 3 and 5).


The key judgment problem is not whether one service, one source, or one document is automatically correct. The clinician must weigh when each source was written, what evidence it used, what it can and cannot answer, and how the patient is actually trending at the discharge-planning snapshot.

4. World Summary
Korvin Merrow presents a familiar but unforgiving inpatient transition: an older, medically complex patient improves after admission, yet safe discharge remains genuinely uncertain. The world tests whether a reviewer can separate medical stabilization from discharge readiness by synthesizing source reliability, consultant timing, medication actions, objective trends, bedside function, and family baseline knowledge.
The trap architecture works because the most visible documents are the most reassuring, while the decisive evidence sits in less prominent sources: a polished discharge snapshot, evolving consultant addenda, an unresolved prednisone record, and buried nursing and therapy observations. The world is hard for an AI agent because it rewards exactly what the chart punishes: confident single-source summarization, copy-forward narration, and premature closure, when the correct behavior is date-aware cross-document reconciliation and restraint from inventing outcomes. A strong response reasons the way a careful hospitalist would: weighing every voice, trusting trends over snapshots, and leaving ambiguous what the record leaves ambiguous.

Terminology: Traps vs. Frictions
Traps are informational obstacles embedded in the source files that test the model's ability to reason through noisy, contradictory, or incomplete data. Examples: contradictory medication doses across sources, buried significant findings, missing information requiring acknowledged uncertainty, source-of-truth ambiguity, temporal complexity (cause cannot postdate effect), SDOH affecting clinical reasoning.

Friction points are people/perspective conflicts that test the model's ability to navigate competing viewpoints and authority. Examples: specialty A vs. specialty B recommendations, family preference vs. clinical team, PCP plan vs. inpatient discharge, payer policy vs. clinical indication, regulatory framework A vs. framework B.These terms are not interchangeable. A world should have both. Traps live in the data; frictions live in the relationships between stakeholders or frameworks.

Self-containment principle
Every correct task response must be traceable to the prompt, its task-level context files, and the world-level file set. Supplementary files can add realism and background, but no task should depend on a supplementary file as the sole source of critical evidence.
Self-Containment Principle
Every claim in a task's correct answer must be traceable to the task prompt + task-level files + world-level files. If a clinical fact, date, diagnosis, or regulatory finding cannot be accounted for by those sources, it is either fabrication (if asserted as fact) or must be acknowledged as uncertain. This principle governs golden response construction, grader guideline calibration, and the accountability test applied during QA.
