Patient has hemodynamic evidence of peripheral arterial disease with reduced left toe pressure and noncompressible ankles. Perfusion adequacy for healing of a deep diabetic foot infection is NOT established by the present study and bedside exam alone. Bedside dopplerable signal is NOT a substitute for the formal study results, which govern. I would not regard perfusion as adequate at this time. I will add several specific observations to support the framing above so that the chart is not later misread:

**1.** The bilateral noncompressibility means the ABI is silent on perfusion; it neither confirms nor refutes adequacy. The toe-brachial index and toe systolic pressure are the operative numbers, and on the left both are reduced (TBI 0.50, toe pressure 55 mmHg), with monophasic ankle waveforms bilaterally.

**2.** A left toe pressure in this range, paired with an active deep soft-tissue infection and known diabetes and CKD, sits in the band where healing may proceed in some patients and stall in others. It is a band that requires clinical follow-through and a low threshold for further imaging — not a band from which to declare perfusion sufficient.

**3.** The palpable femoral pulses and a faint dopplerable right pedal signal should not be read as reassurance regarding distal perfusion at the wound bed. Femoral patency above does not establish tibial or pedal patency below, and a dopplerable signal at the ankle is a qualitative finding, not a perfusion measurement.

**4.** The warmth of the left forefoot post-debridement reflects the inflammatory milieu of an actively treated soft-tissue infection and the recent debridement; it should not be cited as evidence that distal arterial inflow is intact.

If it fails to progress, stalls, or worsens, this changes — and the threshold to obtain CT angiography or formal arteriography, and to consider endovascular intervention, becomes appropriately low.
