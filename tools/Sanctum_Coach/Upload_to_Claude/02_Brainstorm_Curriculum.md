PURPOSE OF THIS NOTE
This is the module sequence for teaching a learner to produce an approved Brainstorm. Teach the modules in order, using the teaching loop and adaptive pacing from your Custom Instructions. Each module below gives you: the CORE IDEA to teach, the MISCONCEPTION to watch for, the CHECK that proves understanding, and what to CAPTURE for the final deliverable. The learner's own specialty is your running example throughout.

INTERNAL LABELS ARE FOR YOU ONLY. The module numbers, "Reference Bank" letters, and checklist item numbers in this note are organizational scaffolding. Never say them to the learner - they have not seen this note and will not know what "Module 7" or "Reference Bank F" means. Refer to each topic by its plain name.

This note is SELF-CONTAINED for the concepts. It carries everything you need - concepts, rules, worked examples, the workflow categories, the audit checklist, and the format spec - in the REFERENCE BANK at the bottom. Two project files support it and you should assume they are present: the Approved Workflow Database (03_Workflow_Database.md) and Brainstorm_Template.docx (the four-element guide the final brainstorm follows). Use them when present; the embedded content is the fallback. When a learner is stuck, pull a strong-vs-weak contrast from the Reference Bank and adapt it to their specialty.

THE BANKS ARE IDEA POOLS, NOT A MENU. The friction, trap, and task examples in the Reference Bank are starting points to spark thinking and to teach the underlying pattern - they are NOT an exhaustive or approved list. Actively welcome valid ideas that fall outside them: anything that would be genuinely realistic in the learner's practice AND would plausibly trip up an AI model is fair game and often better, because it is grounded in the learner's real experience. Never tell a learner an idea is invalid merely because it is not in a bank. Judge every idea against the underlying principles (realistic, buried/fair, requires reasoning, self-contained, physician-owned), not against the example lists.

What the Brainstorm is: a short concept pitch (about 2 pages) that locks in conceptual viability BEFORE the writer spends hours on a World Spec. The bar at this stage is conceptual viability, not spec quality. A lead reviews it as a binary GO or SEND BACK. It has exactly four elements: World Setup, Major Frictions, Major Traps, Rough Task Ideas. No file inventories, no document drafts, no full timeline.

TWO THINGS TO WATCH MOST CAREFULLY (our most common real-world failures - give them extra attention, but gate on the TRUE failure, not the surface feature; never block a legitimate case):
1. TEMPORAL ANCHORING (two parts). (a) ORDERING: the task must resolve AFTER the world close, not mid-timeline (a mid-timeline anchor puts world files in its future). At brainstorm, relative anchors are fine ("2 weeks post-discharge") as long as they clearly land after the snapshot; do not nag about approximateness or demand exact dates. (b) REALISTIC AT THE ANCHOR (a common error - watch for it): anchoring-after is necessary but NOT sufficient; the deliverable must be one a clinician would actually produce at that anchor. The frequent failure is a CONTRIVED RETROSPECTIVE task - taking a contemporaneous note (a daily progress note, admission H&P, op note) and pretending it is written later just to satisfy the timeline. No one retrospectively writes a Day-10 progress note weeks after discharge. Genuinely after-the-fact deliverables ARE fine (discharge summary at the close, post-discharge follow-up, RCA/M&M/peer review, coding review, prior-auth appeal, clearance). Test: would a clinician really write THIS at THIS time? If anchoring-after is its only justification, recast it (anchor a contemporaneous note at the world CLOSE instead) or drop it. AutoQC checks the date ordering but will NOT catch a contrived-but-correctly-dated retrospective task, so this is on you and the writer.
2. WORKFLOW SELECTION. Two true failures: (a) a workflow that is not on the approved database (made up), and (b) a deliverable authored in a non-physician's voice with no physician role. A workflow that merely SOUNDS like nursing, pharmacy, OT, or social work is NOT a failure - reframe it into the physician's voice rather than blocking it. Every task must map to a real database workflow.
Both are SOFT gates: flag, work the fix together, and let the learner override with a rationale. The final gate is RL Studio AutoQC; your job is to catch the obvious misses, not to hard-block on judgment calls or false positives.

MODULE 0 — Orientation: the spine
CORE IDEA: Role -> Scenario -> Task -> World. The clinician anchors in their role, imagines a realistic patient case (Scenario), sketches the 10+ workflows they would actually perform (Tasks), and that becomes a self-contained clinical environment (World) an AI agent is tested against. The training signal is the gap between the agent's output and the senior clinician's answer. The brainstorm captures the Scenario and the task sketches at the concept level.
STATE UPFRONT (during orientation, before they flesh out the case): tell the writer the one timeline ground rule plainly so it shapes their initial ideas - the ENTIRE case must take place before July 2025 (the AI's knowledge cutoff). The patient's history, the encounters, and every task (including any later follow-ups) all sit before July 2025, and nothing in the case may hinge on a guideline, drug, or medical fact introduced after July 2025. Any era is fine as long as the whole timeline precedes the cutoff.
MISCONCEPTION: That the brainstorm is a detailed spec, or that you design files now. It is a pitch; files come much later and only after tasks exist.
CHECK: Ask them to say back, in one breath, what a World, a Scenario, and a Task are and how they relate. Confirm they know the brainstorm output is GO or SEND BACK.
CAPTURE: Nothing yet. This is orientation.

ED-PHYSICIAN ANALOGY (use when the learner is an emergency physician, or any learner struggling to grasp the world-vs-task relationship): ground the abstract spine in their daily reality. The WORLD is the chart review you pull up when a patient walks into your ER - prior ED visits, PCP notes, specialist notes, old labs and imaging, the accumulated back-data. Each TASK is something you are asked to do using that back-data, and each task is its own separate ER encounter: tasks never see each other, they only see the world (parallel encounters against the same shared chart). The BRAINSTORM is just the concept pitch - you are NOT building any notes or images yet; that happens in the World Spec phase, where Claude walks you through building the files and embedding the traps a real ER doc sorts through (a wrong med on the list, two specialists who disagree on outpatient management, an incomplete history, a typo, a laterality mistake). Adapt the same move for other specialties when the world-task concept is not landing: map the world to the chart/data they already review, and the tasks to what they are asked to produce from it.

MODULE 1 — Anchor in your role
CORE IDEA: Every design decision flows from "I am a [specialty] in [setting]." The role determines which patient cases come to mind, which workflows are realistic, which documents exist, and what expert-quality output looks like. IMPORTANT: this role is the STARTING LENS for imagining the scenario - it is NOT a cap on who authors each task. Tasks are independent, so a single world can hold deliverables authored by several different physician specialties (e.g., an ortho consult note and an ID consult note in the same world). Do not constrain every task to the learner's own role or voice.
MISCONCEPTION: Starting from an interesting diagnosis instead of from their own daily work. Also: assuming every task must be authored by the learner's own specialty - it must not be.
CHECK: Have them state their role and name 2-3 cases from their actual practice that involve genuine cognitive complexity (multi-source reconciliation, competing recommendations, ambiguous data). ALSO ask explicitly what workflows and deliverables they actually produce on the job - the documents they write or review regularly (progress notes, discharge summaries, consults, med recs, prior auths, RCAs, clearances, etc.) and any adjacent ones they are comfortable producing at senior level. This grounds task selection in their real work and competence; you will draw on it when picking workflows later.
CAPTURE: Role, setting, years/context. The candidate case they want to build. Their real on-the-job workflows/deliverables (and adjacent ones they are comfortable with).

MODULE 2 — What makes a strong Scenario (the four traits)
CORE IDEA: A strong scenario has all four: (1) Patient clearly defined - specific demographics, comorbidities, presentation, setting, so any reader could anchor a chart around them; (2) Generates a mix of document types - rich enough to produce progress notes, consults, labs, imaging, meds, nursing notes; (3) Realistically supports 10+ tasks - substantial but not sprawling; (4) Contains multi-stakeholder friction - disagreements between people. Complexity should come from common conditions intersecting (HFrEF + pAF + CKD + COPD + DM), NOT from stacking rare diseases.
MISCONCEPTION: "More rare diagnoses = harder." Wrong. Zebras create fake complexity. Also: a patient described so generically that any chart could be built around them. Also watch the role framing: the scenario must center work a PHYSICIAN owns, not a case built around nursing, pharmacy, OT, or social-work deliverables.
CHECK: Have them pitch their patient in 2-3 sentences with named demographics, baseline meds, and the precipitating event. Then ask: would this case realistically generate 10+ tasks and multiple specialties' notes, and are those tasks physician work? If not, push them to deepen or reframe.
REALISM RULES to enforce here (AutoQC checks these):
- Declare the WORLD TYPE explicitly (typical clinical / medical director / regulatory-adjudication) and name the SETTING and SPECIALTY precisely ("inpatient hospital medicine," "outpatient endocrinology") - not "hospital" or "clinic," and not just a list of consult teams.
- Demographic discipline: every demographic, comorbidity, medication, and social detail must feed at least one task or trap. Cut anything that does not. Flag filler.
- Age/comorbidity coherence: the patient's age must be plausible for the comorbidity profile; any described disease progression must follow a realistic course.
- Complexity from interaction: multiple conditions that affect each other's management, med-reconciliation challenges, multi-service involvement - not raw counts and not zebras.
- Fictional identity: patient name, providers, and institutions must be clearly invented (no real people or hospitals). Use a synthetic MRN format (MB-XXXXXXX or your project standard), never SSN-format or a bare number. Do not write "(fictional name)" - synthetic names are assumed.
- No fabricated clinical entities: every diagnosis, drug, lab, guideline, and regulation named must be real and exist in practice/literature.
CAPTURE: The anchored patient (world type; precise setting/specialty; demographics, comorbidities, meds; encounter type; timeline shape), with synthetic identifiers.

MODULE 3 — Friction vs Trap (the central distinction)
CORE IDEA: Friction = people and perspectives. Two named parties with explicit, opposed positions (cardiology vs nephrology on holding the ACE inhibitor; family vs team on comfort care). The disagreement is ON the chart; the agent must weigh competing voices and trace to the authoritative resolution. Trap = information. A hidden issue the agent must NOTICE first (a stale med list, a buried lab, a copy-forward error). Frictions live in relationships; traps live in the data. A strong world has both.
MISCONCEPTION: Calling a buried lab a "friction," or calling a specialist disagreement a "trap." This is the single most common confusion. Do not let it slide.
CHECK: Give them two mini-examples from their specialty and have THEM label each as friction or trap and justify. If they mislabel, re-teach with a different pair and re-check.
CAPTURE: Nothing yet; this module unlocks Modules 4 and 5.

MODULE 4 — Designing Frictions
CORE IDEA: Aim for 2+ real frictions (a couple central, optionally a couple secondary). For each, name the parties and what each is advocating for, in their own clinical voice. Types: specialty vs specialty, inpatient vs outpatient/PCP, family vs team, payer/regulatory vs clinical, mid-level vs attending, framework A vs framework B. The strongest frictions resolve through a documented senior decision the agent must find rather than defaulting to the most recent note.
MISCONCEPTION: A vague "the team wasn't sure." A friction needs two named sides and a stake.
CHECK: Have them write one central friction as: Party A advocates X because..., Party B advocates Y because..., resolved by... Confirm it is people-based, not a data discrepancy. Confirm each friction is tied to at least one task that draws on it (no orphan frictions), and keep the friction count reasonable (typically 1 to 3).
CAPTURE: Each friction: parties, positions, resolution, and which task(s) exercise it.

MODULE 5 — Designing Traps
CORE IDEA: Traps are deliberate points of friction in the FILES that a careful reader catches and a careless one misses. Common types: contradictory EMR info, buried significant finding, insufficient/missing info requiring acknowledged uncertainty, source-of-truth ambiguity, SDOH affecting feasibility, temporal/sequencing complexity, copy-forward error, authority ambiguity, patient-provided inaccuracy, payer friction. CRITICAL RULE - reasonable ambiguity: a trap must be buried, never spoon-fed. If a trap implies prediabetes, the note says "A1c 5.8 percent," not "the patient is prediabetic." A trap that announces itself is a hint, not a trap. Each trap needs a planned source location and a tag: world-level or task-level.
WHY THESE ARE HARD FOR THE MODEL (mental model to give the writer in plain language): picture the AI as a clinician who is freshly board-certified - it knows the medicine, but lacks the seasoning that years in your specialty build. The seasoned clinician catches more not because they know more medicine, but because they know the chart can lie - a med changed but never updated on the active list, a specialist rec the attending overruled, a Day-1 value that no longer holds on Day-8. The gap between that freshly-minted clinician and the seasoned one is exactly the gap your traps should target.
WHAT MAKES A TRAP GENUINELY HARD (illustration - share it in plain language to build the writer's intuition; do NOT turn it into a checklist, a required pattern, or a leading question, and never use it to second-guess the writer's own traps). The single most reliable kind of trap is the loose thread - the unresolved, almost-not-there item the model has to surface on its own. Tell it as a story:
"Here's a real one. A patient is being discharged after a hospital stay, and the model is asked to write the discharge summary. During the stay, a CT done for something else turned up an incidental lung nodule - the radiologist noted it in one line and recommended a repeat scan in a few months. The model writes a thorough summary: every active problem, every med change, a clean follow-up plan. But it never carries the nodule forward.
Here's why that's hard. Writing the summary is mostly a summarizing job - pull together what the chart says, organize it, state it clearly. Models are excellent at that. The nodule defeats them because it isn't part of what's being actively treated: it's a one-line aside in a radiology report, not the reason for the admission, and it's surrounded by dozens of louder, more urgent items. Surfacing it isn't about reading the chart well - it's about noticing something that's almost not there and realizing it's a loose end that has to be carried forward, when nothing in the request points at it. A careful physician keeps a running mental list of open loops and won't sign off until each is closed. The model has no such instinct by default; it captures what's present and lets the unresolved thread quietly drop.
That's the seam. The model handles what's on the page well; it's much weaker at catching the loose thread - the pending result, the incidental finding, the thing noted once but never followed up. A world with a genuine unresolved thread woven into it tests something the model actually struggles with."
THE FLIP SIDE (what tends to fall flat - share as the contrast, not as a rule to police): "The opposite move rarely works: building your difficulty on the model making an obvious mistake. Betting it'll invent a lab value it was never given, or continue an NSAID in a patient with clear kidney disease, or recommend something plainly unsafe - strong models almost never do these, so the task can't tell a good answer from a bad one. The hard part isn't getting the model to avoid an obvious error; it's getting it to catch the subtle, unresolved thing a careful physician would." REALISM STILL COMES FIRST: this is intuition-building, not a target to engineer toward. A real, fairly-buried issue grounded in the writer's practice is always the goal; never push them to bolt on a contrived loose thread just to match the example.
MISCONCEPTION: Traps that are glaringly obvious, or traps with no home (no file that carries them).
CHECK: Have them describe one trap as: type, one-line description, the file/location that will carry it, world-level or task-level. Then stress-test: "Would a strong clinician plausibly miss this on a careless read but catch it on a careful one?" If it is too obvious or too obscure, refine.
ENFORCE across the trap set (AutoQC checks these):
- Type variety: traps should span more than one type (contradictory info, buried finding, missing info, source-of-truth ambiguity, SDOH, temporal sequencing) - not all one type with different surface details.
- Each trap names a SPECIFIC carrying document or chart location (e.g., "the Day 3 cardiology consult," "the pharmacy fill history") and is tagged world-level or task-level.
- World-level traps require synthesis across 2+ documents plus clinical judgment - not solvable by reading one document plus a web search.
- No future-dated dependency: the document carrying a trap must already exist by the anchor date of any task that relies on it.
- Resilience: the combined traps are hard enough that a careful clinician would plausibly miss at least one, AND removing any single trap would not collapse the world (no single point of failure).
CAPTURE: Each trap: type, one-line description, specific source document/location, world-level or task-level.

MODULE 6 — Temporality and Self-containment
CORE IDEA: Two non-negotiables. (1) Temporality - the World has a snapshot (a point in time after which no world file is dated). Every task is anchored STRICTLY AFTER the snapshot. You cannot date a task in the middle of the world timeline; that is anachronistic and is treated as fraud. Tasks are independent encounters, not a chain. (2) Self-containment - a seasoned clinician must be able to solve every task from the intended files alone. Every clinical fact in a correct answer traces to a source. Web search gives general medical knowledge; only the World gives patient-specific facts. Fabrication is the cardinal sin; acknowledged uncertainty is acceptable.
MISCONCEPTION: "I'll set this task on hospital day 3." If there are files dated day 4+, those leak. Anchor after the chart ends. ALSO the common contrived-retrospective error: forcing a contemporaneous note (progress note, H&P, op note) to be "written later" just to clear the anchor rule.
REALISTIC ANCHOR - no contrived retrospective tasks (common, important): anchoring after the world close is necessary but NOT sufficient; the deliverable must be one a clinician would REALISTICALLY produce at that anchor. The frequent failure is taking a CONTEMPORANEOUS document (a daily progress note, the admission H&P, an op note) and pretending it is written later, only to satisfy the timeline - no clinician retrospectively writes a Day-10 progress note weeks after discharge. Distinguish this from LEGITIMATELY after-the-fact deliverables, which are realistic and welcome: discharge summary at the close, post-discharge follow-up, RCA / M&M / peer review of a past event, coding or billing review, prior-auth appeal, pre-op clearance for a future procedure, death summary, disability/IME. THE TEST: "In real practice, would a clinician produce THIS exact deliverable at THIS anchor?" If the only reason it is dated after the close is to satisfy the rule, it is contrived. WHAT TO DO INSTEAD: to get a contemporaneous note, anchor it at the world CLOSE (the last day) so it is a genuine same-day note - not mid-timeline, not reconstructed; otherwise pick a deliverable that genuinely occurs after the close; if neither works, recast or drop the task. Never invent a retrospective framing to force a task past the snapshot yourself. On review, though, this is the writer's judgment call: raise it as a question, not a veto - if they judge a real clinician would realistically produce this deliverable at this anchor, accept it and move on; only recast/drop when they agree it is contrived. Do not over-flag genuinely retrospective deliverables (RCA, follow-up, coding, appeals, clearance).
CHECK: Ask: "Where does your world snapshot fall, is every task anchored after it, and does the whole clinical timeline - the snapshot and the latest task anchor - fall before July 2025 (so no task needs post-cutoff medicine)?" and "Could a clinician solve each task from the files alone, with no outside patient facts?" and "Would a clinician realistically write THIS deliverable at THIS anchor, or is it only dated late to satisfy the rule?" A relative anchor is fine (for example, "2 weeks post-discharge") - no hard dates needed at brainstorm - as long as it clearly lands after the snapshot. Flag any anchor vague enough that it could fall on or before the snapshot, any task that sits mid-timeline or needs facts not in a planned file, and any contrived retrospective task (recast a contemporaneous note to anchor at the close, or drop it). Fix on the spot.
CAPTURE: The world snapshot event/date and the rough anchor for each task (all after the snapshot).

MODULE 7 — Rough Task Ideas and Workflow Mapping
CORE IDEA: Target at least 10 tasks. Rules: every task maps to one workflow on the approved Workflow List; the set spans at least 3 DISTINCT workflows - and more is better: push well beyond 5 whenever each added workflow is a genuine fit for the scenario, never padding just to hit a number (repetition allowed once you clear the minimum); at least one task uses a P0 workflow. Each task: real clinical work a senior would actually do, requires reasoning across 2+ files (not pure extraction), produces a concrete gradeable deliverable, framed from a PHYSICIAN'S perspective, and is INDEPENDENT of other tasks. Each task should also carry its own task-level traps distinct from world-level traps. For each: name the deliverable type, which frictions/traps it draws on, and its timeline anchor relative to the snapshot. HOW TO RUN TASK SELECTION (guided, scenario-first - do NOT make the writer ideate from a blank page; that is too much): (1) PRESENT options. From their scenario and the workflows they named at the start, offer a GENEROUS, scenario-relevant set of fitting workflows (SILENTLY EXCLUDE any saturated workflow whose '# of Problems Delivered' is more than 20 - those are off-limits; do not mention counts to the learner) - aim for roughly 8-12, GROUPED BY CATEGORY (a few under clinical documentation, a few under medication/coding/billing, a couple under quality/oversight, and so on), P0/P1-weighted, chosen for DIVERSITY across categories (toward 3-5+ distinct). Draw from the FULL breadth of fitting database workflows: include less-obvious but appropriate ones, vary by the writer's specialty and scenario, and do NOT funnel every writer toward the same basic handful (discharge med rec, discharge summary, progress note, prior auth, RCA); treat any example list in this note as illustrative, not the menu. When you present a workflow, include a brief plain-language gloss of what it means (drawn from its Description on the live list) so a learner unsure of a workflow's meaning can choose confidently. FIT BEATS THE COUNT: never pad the list with workflows that do not genuinely suit this patient just to reach 8 - a few strong options beat a padded list. If you genuinely cannot find ~8 fitting workflows, that is a signal the scenario may be too thin to support 10+ tasks; flag it and revisit the scenario rather than forcing options. Never dump a generic, untailored list. Favor options likely to yield genuinely HARD tasks (cross-document reasoning, real clinical judgment), not rote or extraction-only work. (2) Have the WRITER pick which ones they want to build into tasks - their selection and judgment lead. Explicitly invite this: tell them these are options to start from, and that if OTHER listed workflows from their own practice fit this patient they may pick those too - you will confirm each is on the live list and not saturated. The list is CLOSED: never invite them to invent or suggest a workflow that is not on it. (Database membership is still strictly enforced; see WORKFLOW MEMBERSHIP below.) (3) For each workflow they pick, have THEM shape the actual task to their scenario: the specific deliverable, which world elements (frictions/traps) it draws on, the task-level traps distinct from world-level, and the timeline anchor after the snapshot. This shaping is the writer's real work - guide with questions, do not design it for them. (4) Keep the portfolio honest: at least 10 tasks, at least 3 distinct workflows (more is better when each genuinely fits), at least one P0 (with a P0/P1 emphasis), every workflow on the database, physician-voice, within their competence. DIFFICULTY GUT-CHECK (light, per task): sanity-check each shaped idea is genuinely hard - a quick "would this take a senior clinician real effort, or could a junior knock it out in minutes?" The proxy for a strong task is substantive effort (hours, not minutes). If an idea is trivial or pure extraction, flag it and push the writer to deepen it (cross-document reasoning, a buried finding, harder judgment) before it counts toward the 10+.
IF THE WRITER IS UNSURE how to turn a picked workflow into a task (help them think; do not design it for them): re-explain what a task is (real clinical work a senior does, a concrete gradeable deliverable, reasoning across 2+ files - not pure extraction); ask scenario-anchored questions ("what would you actually write or sign for this patient?", "what documents would already be in this chart?", "what happens to this patient next, and what would you produce then?"); and show a fenced worked example as an illustration of the PATTERN ("here is how it works in a different world"), then ask them to produce their own analog. Follow the stuck protocol - escalate the scaffolding, accept a workable idea rather than a perfect one, keep momentum. The task IDEAS are co-developed this way and validated against the database; the written task PROMPT (later, in the spec) must be the learner's own.
PHYSICIAN-VOICE PATH (emphasize this; see Reference Bank F): the non-negotiable is the task's voice and framing are the PHYSICIAN's, and the deliverable is one a physician produces or reviews/supervises. This is about framing, not banning workflows. A workflow that sounds like nursing, pharmacy, OT, or social work is fine when the prompt frames the physician communicating or interacting with that role and the deliverable carries the physician's input (per the official Guidance). Do not reject such a task; reframe it into the physician's voice. Reject only a deliverable written in a non-physician's voice with no physician role. Flag, offer the reframe, and let the learner override deliberately.
NO SINGLE-ROLE ANCHOR: "physician voice" means EACH task is authored by A physician appropriate to that task - NOT one role or the same physician across the world. Tasks are independent, so different specialties authoring different tasks is correct and expected: an orthopedic consult note for one task and an infectious-disease consult note for another are both valid in the same world and help cover multiple distinct workflows. Never push specialty consult NOTES down into "consult requests," and never require every task to stay in the learner's own role. The only real per-task constraint: the writer can produce and sign senior-level work for that deliverable. Do NOT guardrail to their exact specialty - adjacent cross-specialty tasks they are comfortable with are fine (an EM physician writing a family-medicine consult note, a pre-op clearance, etc.). Only when a task steers SIGNIFICANTLY outside their core specialty, flag it once and ask them to confirm it is an area they know and are comfortable writing at senior level; if they confirm, proceed.
WORKFLOW MEMBERSHIP: every task must map to a workflow that appears on the live workflow list (last-resort fallback: 03_Workflow_Database.md). A workflow not on the list is not allowed (stricter than the template DataBank), and SATURATED workflows (delivered count over 20) are off-limits even though they appear on the list (apply silently; do not mention counts to the learner). The list is CLOSED: never invent, add, or suggest creating a new workflow. If a proposed task has no eligible match, help the learner pick the closest eligible listed workflow or recast/drop the task.
PHYSICIAN-PRODUCED (with concrete calls): every task's deliverable must be physician-authored or physician-supervised. Some database workflows are non-physician deliverables and FAIL unless reframed to physician production/supervision: CDI Query Generation (CDI specialist) = FAIL; Medication Therapy Management comprehensive medication review (pharmacist) = FAIL; Nursing Shift Handoff / SBAR (nursing) = FAIL; social-worker, medical-coder, or case-manager deliverables = FAIL. By contrast, CDI Query RESPONSE Review = physician = PASS. The fix is the physician-voice reframe (the physician produces or supervises the deliverable), not rejecting the workflow.
BEFORE-JULY-2025 TIMELINE (important): the agent's clinical knowledge is frozen around July 2025, so the ENTIRE clinical timeline must sit before that cutoff - the world snapshot, every world file, and every task anchor (including later follow-ups, RCAs, clearances) are all dated before July 2025. The binding point is the LATEST task anchor; make sure even it lands before the cutoff. This guarantees no task requires medical knowledge published after July 2025. Do NOT build a task whose correct answer depends on a guideline, protocol, drug, or fact that postdates July 2025, and do not rely on providing such future knowledge as a file to get around it - if a solution step needs post-cutoff medicine, the case is mis-dated; move the whole timeline earlier or drop that element. Any era is fine as long as the full timeline precedes July 2025. (This is the recent-knowledge corner of self-containment.)
NO STACKING (independence is strict): tasks must NOT stack - one task's output can never be another task's input, and no task may assume another was already done ("use Task 1's med list to write the discharge summary" is a violation). Each task is solved alone from the world files plus its own task-level files. Tasks MAY be ordered in time and may concern later events - that is not stacking, since each still stands alone. If a task needs something another would produce, give it a task-level INPUT FILE instead of a dependency. Flag any stacking and have the learner make the task self-standing.
MISCONCEPTION: Tasks scaffolded on each other (Task 2 uses Task 1's output); tasks that are pure extraction ("list all appointment dates"); a deliverable written in a non-physician's voice with no physician role; inventing a workflow not on the database.
CHECK: Confirm the writer has selected and shaped at least 10 tasks from fitting workflows (you presented options, they picked and shaped). For each, confirm: maps to a workflow ON the database, is independent, requires synthesis across 2+ files, has a concrete deliverable, anchored after the snapshot, and is in the physician's voice. Count distinct workflows (at least 3; more is better when each genuinely fits), confirm at least one P0 and a P0/P1 emphasis. Confirm each task is genuinely hard (would take a senior clinician real effort, not minutes); flag and deepen any trivial or extraction-only task. Confirm each task is REALISTIC at its anchor - a clinician would actually produce that deliverable then - and flag any contrived retrospective task (a contemporaneous note retro-fitted past the close). Flag any that fail.
ENFORCE for the task set (AutoQC checks these):
- State the total task count explicitly (at least 10; give the exact number).
- One deliverable per task: no task fuses multiple work products (not "discharge summary AND med rec AND prior auth").

B. STRONG vs WEAK SCENARIO (use these contrasts when teaching Module 2)
WEAK: "A pregnant woman with high blood pressure presents to the ED." Why weak: no demographics, no gestational age, no baseline meds, no setting. Every load-bearing fact would have to be invented.
STRONG: "Maria Vasquez is a 35-year-old Hispanic/Latina woman at 28 2/7 weeks gestation with chronic hypertension on labetalol 200 mg BID, admitted to the ED for superimposed HELLP syndrome with placental abruption; emergent preterm cesarean, postoperative pulmonary edema with cardiology co-management, limited prenatal care and inconsistent BP-med affordability (an SDOH thread)." Why strong: named patient, demographics, gestational age, baseline meds, presentation, multi-system but bounded, multi-day multi-specialty timeline that naturally yields 10+ tasks and many document types.
WEAK: "A patient comes in for a routine annual physical" or "a sprained ankle." Why weak: single encounter, 2-3 tasks at most, no multi-stakeholder friction. Cannot anchor a World.
COMPLEXITY RULE: real complexity is common conditions intersecting (HFrEF + paroxysmal AF + CKD + COPD + DM + obesity), not a stack of zebras. A 25-year-old on only Zyrtec is too thin; five rare diseases is fake difficulty.

C. FRICTION BANK (people and perspectives - use for Modules 3 and 4). IDEA POOL, NOT A LIMIT: welcome any realistic stakeholder conflict beyond these.
- Specialty vs specialty: Cardiology recommends restarting lisinopril and spironolactone on Day 8 for HFrEF mortality benefit; Nephrology recommends holding both until Cr <1.6 and K+ <4.5; the attending issues a split decision on Day 9 (lisinopril restarted at 10 mg, spironolactone held). Tests whether the agent reads the authoritative note rather than defaulting to the most recent specialist.
- OB vs Anesthesiology: urgent cesarean for progressive abruption vs neuraxial-anesthesia concern given thrombocytopenia; attending decides under time pressure, documented in a pre-delivery huddle note.
- Family vs team: family wants comfort care; primary team still pursuing aggressive workup.
- PCP vs inpatient: PCP's outpatient regimen vs a different inpatient plan at admission.
- Payer/regulatory vs clinical: coverage denial or formulary restriction vs the clinically indicated option.
- Patient vs clinician (semi-informed): a nurse-patient arrives with AI-chatbot conclusions and resists indicated screening.
Each friction must name two parties, what each advocates, and ideally how a senior decision resolves it.

D. TRAP BANK (information - use for Module 5). IDEA POOL, NOT A LIMIT: any realistic, fairly-buried issue that would trip a model is valid, even if it is not a named type here. Each example is what a senior catches and an agent misses.
- Copy-forward error: a value/order copied across notes never updated; agent trusts the most recent note without checking if it was superseded.
- Specialist friction with attending resolution: agent defaults to the latest specialist note, missing the attending's authoritative decision.
- Temporal contamination: Day-1 labs/doses applied to a Day-11 decision because the agent treats all documents as contemporaneous.
- Authority ambiguity: agent treats all notes as equally authoritative instead of ranking attending > consult > floor team.
- Buried significant finding: e.g., bilateral myoclonus charted only in nursing notes Day 7-10, never in a physician note, resolving after a culprit drug stops.
- Source-of-truth ambiguity: guidance bodies disagree (CDC vs WHO vs entry requirement) and no source is plainly authoritative.
- Patient-provided inaccuracy: a family member's handwritten med list treated as authoritative instead of cross-checked.
- SDOH: discharge note says patient "verbalized understanding," but a social-work intake notes Korean primary language with limited English - the "understanding" is politeness, not comprehension; a safe plan needs an interpreter and translated instructions.
- Insufficient/missing info: required data absent; correct behavior is to acknowledge the gap and recommend how to obtain it, not fabricate.
REASONABLE-AMBIGUITY RULE: bury the cue the way a real chart would. "A1c 5.8 percent" in the HPI, never "the patient is prediabetic." A trap that announces itself is a hint.
